Appended Statements I / XICI-U
XICI-U · 25 units · text_version: yizhuan-en-3.0.0 · review_status: draft
UnitOriginalTranslation
第一章 第 1 段XICI-U-01-01天尊地卑，乾坤定矣。卑高以陳，貴賤位矣。動靜有常，剛柔斷矣。方以類聚，物以羣分，吉凶生矣。在天成象，在地成形，變化見矣。是故剛柔相摩，八卦相荡，鼓之以雷霆，潤之以風雨，日月運行，一寒一暑。乾道成男，坤道成女；乾知大始，坤作成物；乾以易知，坤以簡能；易則易知，簡則易從；易知則有親，易從則有功；有親則可久，有功則可大；可久則賢人之德，可大則賢人之業。易簡，而天下之理得矣。天下之理得，而成位乎其中矣。
The passage begins with heaven above and earth below as a way of describing the distinct but related roles of Qian and Kun. Movement and stillness, strength and yielding, and the grouping of things by kind make differences and change visible. Thunder, rain, wind, sunlight, and the alternating seasons give concrete examples. Qian is associated with initiating life, while Kun brings things into form. The author then emphasizes ease and simplicity: what is clear can be understood and trusted, and what is straightforward can be practiced effectively. Understanding these principles allows people to find their place within the larger order of heaven and earth.
第二章 第 1 段XICI-U-02-01聖人設卦觀象，繫辭焉而明吉凶；剛柔相推而生變化。是故吉凶者，失得之象也。悔吝者，憂虞之象也。變化者，進退之象也。剛柔者，晝夜之象也。六爻之動，三極之道也。是故君子所居而安者，易之序也。所樂而玩者，爻之辭也。是故君子居則觀其象而玩其辭，動則觀其變而玩其占；是以自天祐之，吉无不利。
The sages established hexagrams by observing images and added statements to distinguish favorable and unfavorable situations. As firm and yielding lines interact, circumstances change. Good fortune and misfortune describe gain and loss; regret and difficulty warn of emerging problems; advance and retreat are different expressions of change. The six lines connect patterns associated with heaven, earth, and human life. A thoughtful person studies the arrangement of the Changes in quiet times and attends to the words attached to each line. When action is required, the person also considers changing lines and divination, seeking a responsible response rather than treating one judgment as unchangeable.
第三章 第 1 段XICI-U-03-01彖者，言乎象者也。爻者，言乎變者也。吉凶者，言乎其失得也。悔吝者，言乎其小疵也。无咎者，善補過也。是故列貴賤者存乎位，齊小大者存乎卦，辯吉凶者存乎辭，憂悔吝者存乎介，震无咎者存乎悔。是故卦有小大，辭有險易，辭也者，各指其所之。
This passage explains the different functions of the text. The judgment on a hexagram describes its overall image, while individual lines show movement and changing conditions. Good or bad fortune concerns possible gains and losses; regret and difficulty point to smaller faults or warning signs. “No blame” does not mean that mistakes never happen: it means that errors can be recognized and repaired appropriately. Positions distinguish higher and lower roles, while the hexagram as a whole shows the scale of the situation. Its words may sound reassuring or dangerous because they point toward different possible outcomes. Interpretation must therefore consider position, context, and the direction of change together.
第四章 第 1 段XICI-U-04-01易與天地準，故能彌綸天地之道。仰以觀於天文，俯以察於地理，是故知幽明之故。原始反終，故知死生之說。精氣為物，遊魂為變，是故知鬼神之情狀。與天地相似，故不違；知周乎萬物，而道濟天下，故不過；旁行而不流，樂天知命，故不憂；安土敦乎仁，故能愛；範圍天地之化而不過，曲成萬物而不遺，通乎晝夜之道而知，故神无方而易无體。
The Changes is presented as a way of understanding patterns in heaven, earth, and human existence. Looking upward to celestial phenomena and downward to the terrain reveals both visible and hidden processes. Reflecting on beginnings and endings encourages thought about life and death; references to vital energy, wandering spirits, and divine beings reflect the ancient intellectual world of the text. Its ideal is not to withdraw from people but to understand many things without excess, act with compassion, and remain at ease with one's circumstances. Change has no single permanent shape: like day becoming night, its operations must be recognized through the particular situation rather than reduced to one fixed formula.
第五章 第 1 段XICI-U-05-01一陰一陽之謂道：繼之者善也，成之者性也；仁者見之謂之仁，知者見之謂之知；百姓日用而不知，故君子之道鮮矣。顯諸仁，藏諸用，鼓萬物而不與聖人同憂，盛德大業至矣哉！富有之謂大業，日新之謂盛德，生生之謂易，成象之謂乾，效法之謂坤，極數知來之謂占，通變之謂事，陰陽不測之謂神。
The familiar expression “one yin and one yang is called the Way” describes reality as an ongoing relationship between complementary forces. What carries this movement forward is called goodness, and what becomes established within living beings is called their nature. Different observers may recognize compassion or wisdom in the same process, while everyday users may not notice it at all. The passage links great achievement with abundance and great virtue with continual renewal. “Change” means life generating more life. Qian forms initiating images; Kun follows and completes them. Divination explores what may come, practical affairs require adaptability, and the mysterious character of yin and yang is described as spirit.
第六章 第 1 段XICI-U-06-01夫易，廣矣！大矣！以言乎遠則不禦，以言乎邇則靜而正，以言乎天地之間則備矣！夫乾，其靜也專，其動也直，是以大生焉。夫坤，其靜也翕，其動也闢，是以廣生焉。廣大配天地，變通配四時，陰陽之義配日月，易簡之善配至德。
The Changes is called vast because its patterns apply both to remote and nearby things. Considered on a grand scale, it reaches beyond any obvious boundary; considered closely, it can be quiet, precise, and orderly. Qian is described as concentrated when still and direct when active, the source of great beginnings. Kun gathers when still and opens when active, giving wide scope for things to develop. These two qualities complement each other rather than compete. The text compares their breadth to heaven and earth, their adaptability to the seasons, and yin and yang to the sun and moon. Ease and simplicity are treated as expressions of exceptional virtue.
第七章 第 1 段XICI-U-07-01子曰：「易其至矣乎！夫易，聖人所以崇德而廣業也，知崇禮卑，崇效天，卑法地。天地設位，而易行乎其中矣。成性存存，道義之門。」
Confucius is quoted as praising the Changes as a profound resource for cultivating character and enlarging worthy undertakings. Wisdom should aspire upward, like heaven, while proper conduct should remain humble, like earth. The point is not to choose intellect over courtesy or humility over achievement. A mature person needs both: expansive understanding and grounded behavior. Heaven and earth each have their position, and the movement of change unfolds between them. By repeatedly preserving and refining one's own moral nature, a person enters the path of duty and right conduct. The passage thus approaches the Changes as a guide to ethical development, not merely a collection of predictions.
第八章 第 1 段XICI-U-08-01聖人有以見天下之賾，而擬諸其形容，象其物宜，是故謂之象。聖人有以見天下之動，而觀其會通，以行其典禮，繫辭焉以斷其吉凶，是故謂之爻。言天下之至賾而不可惡也，言天下之至動而不可亂也。擬之而後言，議之而後動，擬議以成其變化。
The sages perceived complexities in the world and fashioned images resembling the appropriate features of things. Such images became the symbolic language of the Changes. They also observed movement, relationships, and recurring patterns; the lines and their statements describe these developments and distinguish favorable from unfavorable directions. The text claims that even difficult realities can be represented without becoming impossible to understand, and constant movement can be discussed without losing order. Its closing advice is practical: compare and examine a situation before speaking, and deliberate before acting. Careful interpretation and discussion help people respond to change instead of reacting to appearances alone.
第八章 第 2 段XICI-U-08-02「鳴鶴在陰，其子和之。我有好爵，吾與爾靡之。」子曰：「君子居其室，出其言善，則千里之外應之，況其邇者乎！居其室，出其言不善，則千里之外違之，況其邇者乎！言出乎身加乎民；行發乎邇見乎遠。言行，君子之樞機；樞機之發，榮辱之主也。言行，君子之所以動天地也，可不慎乎！」「同人，先號咷而後笑。」子曰：「君子之道，或出或處，或默或語。二人同心，其利斷金。同心之言，其臭如蘭。」
A crane calling from a hidden place is answered by its young, suggesting that genuine words can be heard far beyond their source. The passage explains that good speech may inspire people even a thousand miles away, while harmful speech can drive them away. Words and actions are a pivot on which honor and disgrace turn, so they demand care. The second quotation, from Fellowship with Others, moves from crying to laughter. People may serve or withdraw, speak or remain silent, yet two people who truly share one purpose can overcome formidable obstacles. Their agreement is likened to a fragrance as pleasing as orchids.
第八章 第 3 段XICI-U-08-03「初六，藉用白茅，无咎。」子曰：「苟錯諸地而可矣。藉之用茅，何咎之有！慎之至也。夫茅之為物薄而用可重也，慎斯術也以往，其无所失矣。」
The line about placing an offering on white thatch illustrates exceptional care. An offering could simply be set on the ground, yet placing a thin layer of clean grass beneath it shows additional respect. The grass itself is ordinary and inexpensive, but its use in the right setting carries weight. The lesson is not that elaborate ceremony is always necessary. Rather, attention to a small detail can reveal the seriousness with which an important duty is undertaken. If this attitude of thoughtful preparation is extended to other activities, unnecessary errors become less likely. The text connects prudence in a simple ritual act with steadiness in broader conduct.
第八章 第 4 段XICI-U-08-04「勞謙，君子有終，吉。」子曰：「勞而不伐，有功而不德，厚之至也；語以其功下人者也。德言盛，禮言恭；謙也者，致恭以存其位者也。」
The passage interprets the line about a hardworking yet modest person who reaches a good conclusion. Such a person has accomplishments but does not advertise them, and contributes without claiming every result as personal merit. The text calls this depth of character. Virtue concerns the fullness of one's inner qualities, while ritual propriety concerns the respectful manner in which those qualities are expressed. Modesty does not erase achievement; it prevents success from turning into arrogance. Someone who can lower themselves in relation to others despite real accomplishments is better able to preserve trust and standing. The line therefore links sustained success with humility rather than display.
第八章 第 5 段XICI-U-08-05「亢龍有悔。」子曰：「貴而无位，高而无民，賢人在下位而无輔，是以動而有悔也。」
The expression “a dragon that rises too high has cause for regret” is explained through the isolation of an exalted person. Someone may appear powerful yet lack a secure role, stand above others without public support, and fail to receive help from capable people below. Height alone is not effective leadership. When a person is cut off from colleagues, advisers, and those affected by decisions, even a seemingly strong position becomes unstable. Acting forcefully under such conditions is likely to bring regret. The image of the overreaching dragon warns about the danger of success carried beyond its proper limits, not about ambition in every circumstance.
第八章 第 6 段XICI-U-08-06「不出戶庭，无咎。」子曰：「亂之所生也，則言語以為階。君不密則失臣，臣不密則失身，幾事不密則害成；是以君子慎密而不出也。」
The advice not to step beyond the inner courtyard is connected here with discretion in speech. Disorder often begins when words disclose matters that should have remained confidential. If a ruler cannot keep confidence, ministers may be lost; if a minister is indiscreet, personal safety may be endangered; if sensitive plans leak too early, a valuable undertaking may fail. The passage does not praise silence in every situation. It distinguishes the right time to speak from situations requiring careful restraint. A cultivated person weighs responsibility, audience, and timing before sharing information. Guarding words becomes part of preserving trust and enabling important work to reach completion.
第八章 第 7 段XICI-U-08-07子曰：「作易者，其知盜乎！易曰：『負且乘，致寇至。』負也者，小人之事也。乘也者，君子之器也。小人而乘君子之器，盜思奪之矣。上慢下暴，盜思伐之矣。慢藏誨盜，冶容誨淫。易曰：『負且乘，致寇至。』盜之招也。」
This passage uses the image of someone carrying a burden while riding a distinguished carriage to discuss social roles and the attraction of thieves. In its ancient hierarchy, a carriage could signal status that the rider had not earned, inviting others to seize it. Arrogance above and aggression below can likewise provoke conflict. The text adds that careless storage attracts theft and ostentatious display invites improper attention. These claims reflect historical moral assumptions and should not be taken as excuses for wrongdoing. The central interpretive point is that visible behavior, mismatched responsibilities, and neglected precautions can create avoidable risks. The Changes asks readers to examine the conditions surrounding trouble.
第九章 第 1 段XICI-U-09-01天一地二，天三地四，天五地六，天七地八，天九地十。天數五，地數五，五位相得而各有合。天數二十有五，地數三十，凡天地之數，五十有五，此所以成變化，而行鬼神也。大衍之數五十，其用四十有九，分而為二以象兩，掛一以象三，揲之以四以象四時，歸奇於扐以象閏，五歲再閏，故再扐而後掛。
The passage lays out an ancient numerical account of divination. Odd numbers one, three, five, seven, and nine belong to heaven and total twenty-five; even numbers two, four, six, eight, and ten belong to earth and total thirty. Together they make fifty-five. In the great expansion method, fifty yarrow stalks are counted but forty-nine are used. They are divided into two groups to symbolize the two primary powers; one stalk is set aside to represent a third element. Counting in fours evokes the four seasons, and the remainders evoke intercalary months. These are symbolic procedures within the classical divination system, not a description of modern probability theory.
第九章 第 2 段XICI-U-09-02乾之策，二百一十有六，坤之策，百四十有四，凡三百有六十，當期之日。二篇之策，萬有一千五百二十，當萬物之數也。是故四營而成易，十有八變而成卦，八卦而小成；引而伸之，觸類而長之，天下之能事畢矣。顯道神德行，是故可與酬酢，可與祐神矣。子曰：「知變化之道者，其知神之所為乎！」
Here the text calculates the number of yarrow-stalk values associated with Qian and Kun. Qian's six lines amount to 216, and Kun's to 144, making 360, a number compared with the days of a traditional year. Across the two parts of the Changes, the total is given as 11,520, symbolically associated with the multitude of things. Four operations complete a change, and eighteen changes produce a hexagram. From the eight foundational trigrams, the method can be extended to many combinations and situations. The author treats numerical procedures as a disciplined way to disclose patterns of transformation, while the invocation of spirits belongs to its ancient worldview.
第十章 第 1 段XICI-U-10-01易有聖人之道四焉：以言者尚其辭，以動者尚其變，以制器者尚其象，以卜筮者尚其占。是以君子將有為也，將有行也，問焉而以言，其受命也如響，无有遠近幽深，遂知來物。非天下之至精，其孰能與於此！
The text identifies four ways the sages use the Changes. For speech and interpretation, they value its written statements; for action, they attend to transformation; for designing objects, they study images; and for divination, they consider the resulting judgments. A cultivated person who faces a decision can consult the system as though asking a question and receiving an answering echo. Its traditional description emphasizes that distance or obscurity need not prevent a meaningful response. This is a claim within the classical theory of divination, not proof that every future event can be predicted. The broader lesson is that words, images, changing circumstances, and practical choices serve distinct but connected roles.
第十章 第 2 段XICI-U-10-02參伍以變，錯綜其數，通其變，遂成天下之文，極其數，遂定天下之象；非天下之至變，其孰能與於此！
The passage describes combining and recombining numbers to perceive transformation. By comparing patterns and arranging them in different relationships, the diviner seeks the structures that make phenomena intelligible. Extending numerical operations to their limits is said to establish the images through which the world can be represented. The text admires the ability of the Changes to hold variation and structure together: the combinations are not arbitrary figures but part of an ordered symbolic system. It calls this extraordinary adaptability. In reading the passage, it is useful to distinguish its historical philosophical claim about numerical symbolism from a modern scientific claim about the causes of events.
第十章 第 3 段XICI-U-10-03易，无思也，无為也，寂然不動，感而遂通天下之故。非天下之至神，其孰能與於此！
The Changes is described as having neither private thoughts nor deliberate actions. It remains still and silent, yet when approached through its method, it is said to respond and disclose the underlying reasons for things. The emphasis is on the contrast between inactivity and meaningful response. Unlike a human adviser, the classic is not imagined as pursuing interests or making choices of its own. In the traditional account, its symbolic arrangement becomes informative through the encounter between a question and a changing pattern. The author calls this mysterious, even divine. Readers can understand the passage as expressing the perceived depth of divination without treating the claim as an established physical mechanism.
第十章 第 4 段XICI-U-10-04夫易，聖人之所以極深而研幾也！唯深也，故能通天下之志；唯幾也，故能成天下之務；唯神也，故不疾而速，不行而至。
The Changes is portrayed as a means by which sages investigate both the deepest principles and the first, almost invisible signs of events. Because their inquiry reaches deeply, they can understand people's intentions; because they notice early indications, they can address difficult affairs before they grow. Its work is described as so subtle that it seems to act quickly without haste and arrive without travel. These striking phrases celebrate insight into connected processes, not literal movement across space. The passage joins patient study with timely action. It suggests that a decision becomes more effective when one understands broad conditions and recognizes small changes before they become obvious.
第十章 第 5 段XICI-U-10-05子曰「易有聖人之道四焉」者，此之謂也。
This short sentence looks back to the earlier statement that the Changes contains four ways of the sages. Those four are the use of words to express meaning, the use of transformations to guide action, the use of images in forming objects, and the use of divinatory judgments in consultation. The author is closing an argument rather than introducing a new list. The surrounding discussion explains why each function matters: language clarifies, change reveals timing, images connect ideas with forms, and divination gives a traditional framework for considering uncertainty. The sentence should therefore be read as a conclusion to the preceding passage, not as an independent prediction.
第十一章 第 1 段XICI-U-11-01子曰：「夫易，何為者也？夫易，開物成務，冒天下之道，如斯而已者也。」是故聖人以通天下之志，以定天下之業，以斷天下之疑。是故蓍之德圓而神，卦之德方以知，六爻之義易以貢；聖人以此洗心，退藏於密，吉凶與民同患，神以知來，知以藏往，其孰能與此哉？古之聦明叡知神武而不殺者夫！是以明於天之道，而察於民之故，是興神物以前民用；聖人以此齊戒以神明其德夫！是故闔戶謂之坤，闢戶謂之乾，一闔一闢謂之變；往來不窮謂之通，見乃謂之象，形乃謂之器，制而用之謂之法，利用出入，民咸用之謂之神。是故易有太極，是生兩儀，兩儀生四象，四象生八卦，八卦定吉凶，吉凶生大業。是故法象莫大乎天地；變通莫大乎四時；縣象著明莫大乎日月；崇高莫大乎富貴；備物致用，立成器以為天下利，莫大乎聖人；探賾索隱，鉤深致遠，以定天下之吉凶，成天下之亹亹者，莫大乎蓍龜。是故天生神物，聖人則之。天地變化，聖人效之。天垂象，見吉凶，聖人象之。河出圖，洛出書，聖人則之。易有四象，所以示也。繫辭焉，所以告也。定之以吉凶，所以斷也。
This long passage asks what the Changes is for and answers that it opens understanding, helps accomplish affairs, and embraces the patterns of the world. It describes yarrow stalks, hexagrams, and six lines as complementary ways of examining uncertainty. The sages are said to purify their minds and share the people's concern for fortune and misfortune. Closing a door symbolizes Kun; opening it symbolizes Qian; their alternation is change, and ceaseless movement is continuity. From the Supreme Ultimate arise two modes, then four images and eight trigrams. Instruments, laws, and human undertakings emerge as patterns are given practical form. References to spirits, the River Chart, and the Luo Writing belong to the classical tradition.
第十二章 第 1 段XICI-U-12-01易曰：「自天祐之，吉无不利」。子曰：「祐者，助也。天之所助者，順也。人之所助者，信也。履信思乎順，又以尚賢也，是以『自天祐之，吉无不利。』也。」
“Helped by heaven, auspicious, with nothing unfavorable” is explained through the meaning of help itself. According to the passage, heaven assists those who act in accord with its patterns, while people assist those who can be trusted. A person should therefore cultivate sincerity, think carefully about the direction of events, and honor capable and virtuous people. Assistance is not presented simply as an arbitrary gift. It arises from fitting one's conduct to a wider order and establishing reliable relationships. The language of heaven's support reflects the text's moral cosmology; its practical emphasis is on trustworthiness, responsiveness, and respect for good judgment.
第十二章 第 2 段XICI-U-12-02子曰：「書不盡言，言不盡意。」然則聖人之意，其不可見乎？子曰：「聖人立象以盡意，設卦以盡情偽，繫辭以盡其言，變而通之以盡利，鼓之舞之以盡神。」
Confucius is quoted as saying that writing cannot express every spoken thought and speech cannot exhaust everything in the mind. Does that mean the sages' intentions are inaccessible? The passage answers no. The sages established images to carry meanings beyond literal language, arranged hexagrams to show the complexity of human feelings and appearances, and attached statements to make their teaching clearer. They then explored how change could be understood and applied. Images, words, and transformations work together rather than replacing one another. The passage is an important account of why the Changes uses symbolic figures alongside written explanations: some insights need more than a verbal definition.
第十二章 第 3 段XICI-U-12-03乾坤，其易之縕邪！乾坤成列，而易立乎其中矣。乾坤毀，則无以見易，易不可見，則乾坤或幾乎息矣。是故形而上者謂之道，形而下者謂之器，化而裁之謂之變，推而行之謂之通，舉而錯之天下之民謂之事業。是故夫象，聖人有以見天下之賾，而擬諸其形容，象其物宜，是故謂之象。聖人有以見天下之動，而觀其會通，以行其典禮，繫辭焉以斷其吉凶，是故謂之爻。極天下之賾者存乎卦；鼓天下之動者存乎辭；化而裁之存乎變；推而行之存乎通；神而明之，存乎其人；默而成之，不言而信，存乎德行。
Qian and Kun are presented as the fundamental conditions that allow the Changes to appear. The text distinguishes the Way, which lies beyond particular physical forms, from concrete vessels and instruments, which take recognizable shape. Adapting and reshaping circumstances is called change; extending an effective pattern into action is called continuity or practical connection. When such understanding benefits people, it becomes an undertaking. The sages form images from complex realities and write line statements about events and their outcomes. Yet the final source of effective interpretation is not the diagram alone. The passage says that profound understanding depends on the person, and trustworthy achievement rests on cultivated character.

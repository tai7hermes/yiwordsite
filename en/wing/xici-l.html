Appended Statements II / XICI-L
XICI-U · 35 units · text_version: yizhuan-en-3.0.0 · review_status: draft
UnitOriginalTranslation
第一章 第 1 段XICI-L-01-01八卦成列，象在其中矣；因而重之，爻在其中矣；剛柔相推，變在其中矣；繫辭焉而命之，動在其中矣。吉凶悔吝者，生乎動者也。剛柔者，立本者也；變通者，趣時者也；吉凶者，貞勝者也。天地之道，貞觀者也；日月之道，貞明者也；天下之動，貞夫一者也。夫乾，確然示人易矣；夫坤，隤然示人簡矣。爻也者，效此者也；象也者，像此者也。爻象動乎內，吉凶見乎外，功業見乎變，聖人之情見乎辭。天地之大德曰生，聖人之大寶曰位，何以守位曰仁，何以聚人曰財，理財正辭，禁民為非曰義。
The eight trigrams present images; stacking them creates six lines through which changing situations may be considered. Firm and yielding lines interact, and judgments of good fortune, misfortune, regret, and difficulty arise through action. Qian exemplifies a clear initiative, while Kun exemplifies receptive simplicity. The passage then connects governance to life: nurturing living things is Heaven and Earth's great virtue, while a ruler preserves legitimate authority through humanity, resources, truthful speech, and justice.
第二章 第 1 段XICI-L-02-01古者包犧氏之王天下也，仰則觀象於天，俯則觀法於地，觀鳥獸之文，與地之宜，近取諸身，遠取諸物；於是始作八卦，以通神明之德，以類萬物之情。作結繩而為罔罟，以佃以漁，蓋取諸離。
The text attributes the invention of the eight trigrams to Fuxi, who observed the sky, land, animals, the human body, and surrounding objects. The trigrams classify patterns seen in nature and human life. It also describes how people made knotted nets for fishing and hunting, associating this practical invention with the Li trigram. The account is a traditional explanation of cultural origins, not a modern archaeological chronology.
第二章 第 2 段XICI-L-02-02包犧氏沒，神農氏作，斲木為耜，揉木為耒，耒耨之利，以教天下，蓋取諸益。日中為市，致天下之民，聚天下之貨，交易而退，各得其所，蓋取諸噬嗑。
After Fuxi, the culture hero Shennong is said to have fashioned wooden ploughing tools and taught agriculture. He also gathered people and goods at a midday market so that exchange could meet practical needs. The narrative associates improved farming with the Yi hexagram and organized exchange with Shi He. It presents inventions as ways of relieving human difficulty and gives hexagram images an explanatory role in cultural memory.
第二章 第 3 段XICI-L-02-03神農氏沒，黃帝、堯、舜氏作，通其變，使民不倦；神而化之，使民宜之；易窮則變，變則通，通則久；是以自天祐之，吉无不利。黃帝、堯、舜垂衣裳而天下治，蓋取諸乾坤。
The account next credits the Yellow Emperor, Yao, and Shun with accommodating social change rather than exhausting people through rigid rule. When a method reaches its limit, it must change; change opens a path, and a workable path can endure. Their orderly government, figured by their hanging garments, is associated with Qian and Kun. This is the familiar principle that adaptation makes continuity possible.
第二章 第 4 段XICI-L-02-04刳木為舟，剡木為楫，舟楫之利以濟不通，致遠以利天下，蓋取諸渙。
People hollowed logs into boats and shaped wood into oars. These implements allowed travelers and goods to cross waters that previously separated communities, extending benefits to distant places. The text links the invention to Huan, a hexagram concerned with dispersing obstructions and restoring communication. The point is practical: a well-designed tool can remove a barrier and connect what had been isolated.
第二章 第 5 段XICI-L-02-05服牛乘馬，引重致遠以利天下，蓋取諸隨。重門擊柝以待暴客，蓋取諸豫。
Tamed cattle and horses could carry heavy loads across long distances, extending the range of human work and travel. The text associates this development with Sui, following or adapting. It also describes fortified gates and night watches with wooden clappers to prepare for intruders, linking these precautions to Yu. Mobility and security appear side by side as complementary requirements of settled communities.
第二章 第 6 段XICI-L-02-06斷木為杵，掘地為臼，臼杵之利，萬民以濟，蓋取諸小過。
The invention of mortar and pestle is described in very concrete terms: wood is cut into a pounding implement, and a hollow is made to receive grain. Grinding and pounding make staple food easier to prepare for many households. The passage associates this useful technique with Xiao Guo. The traditional analogy connects small, carefully directed actions with tangible benefits in everyday life.
第二章 第 7 段XICI-L-02-07弦木為弧，剡木為矢，弧矢之利，以威天下，蓋取諸睽。
A wooden bow was bent into shape and arrows were sharpened for its use. These tools provided an ability to defend or exert power over a distance, changing the balance of force between people. The passage associates their invention with Kui, a hexagram of divergence. It records an ancient account of technology and authority rather than endorsing armed coercion as a present-day moral rule.
第二章 第 8 段XICI-L-02-08上古穴居而野處，後世聖人易之以宮室，上棟下宇，以待風雨，蓋取諸大壯。
The earliest people are pictured as living in caves or in the open. Later generations built houses with beams and roofs that offered shelter from wind and rain. The narrative links the development of durable buildings with Da Zhuang, great strength. It shows how the classical text uses hexagram qualities to illuminate a transition from exposure to organized habitation.
第二章 第 9 段XICI-L-02-09古之葬者，厚衣之以薪，葬之中野，不封不樹，喪期无數，後世聖人易之以棺槨，蓋取諸大過。
The passage contrasts an early burial practice, in which bodies were wrapped in brushwood and placed in the countryside, with later funerary customs using inner and outer coffins. It notes that early graves lacked formal mounds, planted markers, or fixed mourning periods. The transition to more elaborate burial is associated with Da Guo. This is a traditional historical narrative of the growing formality of ritual.
第二章 第 10 段XICI-L-02-10上古結繩而治，後世聖人易之以書契，百官以治，萬民以察，蓋取諸周易/夬。
Knotted cords once helped communities keep records and administer affairs. Later rulers are said to have introduced written signs and contracts, enabling officials to manage duties and ordinary people to verify arrangements. This innovation is associated with Guai, the hexagram of decisive clarification. The underlying thought is that reliable documentation makes public decisions and obligations more understandable.
第三章 第 1 段XICI-L-03-01是故易者，象也。象也者，像也。彖者，材也。爻也者，效天下之動者也。是故吉凶生而悔吝著也。
The text explains why the Changes speaks through images. An image resembles something observed; the Tuan commentary considers the overall capacities of a hexagram, while individual lines imitate movement and changing circumstances. Fortune and misfortune, regret and difficulty, become recognizable within this framework. Rather than treating symbols as literal photographs of events, readers are asked to consider what kind of situation an image represents.
第三章 第 2 段XICI-L-03-02陽卦多陰，陰卦多陽，其故何也？陽卦奇，陰卦偶。其德行何也？陽一君而二民，君子之道也；陰二君而一民，小人之道也。
The passage discusses why certain trigrams are classed as yang despite containing two yin lines, while others are classed as yin despite containing two yang lines. It uses odd and even patterns as its organizing principle. One ruler with two subjects is described as a stable arrangement, while two rulers with one subject illustrates divided authority. These judgments reflect the text's traditional political hierarchy, not a modern claim about people's worth.
第四章 第 1 段XICI-L-04-01易曰：「憧憧往來，朋從爾思。」子曰：「天下何思何慮！天下同歸而殊途，一致而百慮，天下何思何慮！日往則月來，月往則日來，日月相推而明生焉。寒往則暑來，暑往則寒來，寒暑相推而歲成焉。往者，屈也；來者，信也。屈信相感而利生焉。尺蠖之屈，以求信也。龍蛇之蟄，以存身也。精義入神，以致用也。利用安身，以崇德也。過此以往，未之或知也。窮神知化，德之盛也。」
The passage begins by questioning restless speculation. Different roads can reach a shared destination, just as the sun and moon alternate and the seasons succeed one another. Withdrawal and extension work together: a caterpillar contracts before advancing, and snakes and dragons withdraw to preserve life. Understanding the underlying principle is useful only when put into practice. The ideal is to respond skillfully to recurring patterns rather than remain caught in endless worry.
第四章 第 2 段XICI-L-04-02易曰：「困于石，據于蒺蔾，入于其宮，不見其妻，凶。」子曰：「非所困而困焉，名必辱；非所據而據焉，身必危；既辱且危，死期將至，妻其可得見耶！」
Quoting a line about being trapped among rocks and thorns, Confucius warns against clinging to an inappropriate position or relying on what cannot support us. Such choices expose a person to disgrace and danger. The image of returning home unable to find one's wife emphasizes the severity of the consequences. The commentary asks readers to examine whether a chosen place or support is genuinely suitable.
第四章 第 3 段XICI-L-04-03易曰：「公用射隼于高墉之上，獲之，无不利。」子曰：「隼者，禽也；弓矢者，器也。射之者，人也。君子藏器於身，待時而動，何不利之有。動而不括，是以出而有獲，語成器而動者也。」
The image of a nobleman shooting a hawk from a high wall becomes a lesson about preparation and timing. The hawk is the target, the bow and arrow are instruments, and the archer must have the skill to use them. A cultivated person keeps abilities ready and acts only when the opportunity is right. Effective action follows from preparation; hurried movement without the proper means is not the ideal.
第四章 第 4 段XICI-L-04-04子曰：「小人不恥不仁，不畏不義，不見利不勸，不威不懲，小懲而大誡，此小人之福也。易曰：『履校滅趾，无咎。』此之謂也。善不積不足以成名，惡不積不足以滅身，小人以小善為无益而弗為也，以小惡為无傷而弗去也；故惡積而不可揜，罪大而不可解。易曰：『何校滅耳，凶。』」
This passage considers how small habits accumulate. It portrays people who ignore minor wrongdoing unless a penalty forces them to stop, and who dismiss small good actions as insignificant. Yet repeated misconduct eventually becomes difficult to conceal or correct. The images of restraints on toes and ears mark early and late consequences. The moral argument is that correction should begin while an error is still manageable.
第四章 第 5 段XICI-L-04-05子曰：「危者，安其位者也。亡者，保其存者也。亂者，有其治者也。是故君子安而不忘危，存而不忘亡，治而不忘亂，是以身安而國家可保也。易曰：『其亡其亡，繫于苞桑。』」
Prosperity can create the illusion that danger has disappeared. Confucius therefore urges the cultivated person to remember peril while secure, possible loss while thriving, and disorder while governing peacefully. The image of something tied securely to mulberry roots emphasizes vigilance and sound foundations. Stability is maintained not by assuming it will last automatically, but by attending to what might undermine it.
第四章 第 6 段XICI-L-04-06子曰：「德薄而位尊，知小而謀大，力小而任重，鮮不及矣。易曰：『鼎折足，覆公餗，其形渥，凶。』言不勝其任也。」
A person of weak character holding high office, limited knowledge planning vast affairs, or insufficient strength carrying a heavy burden is exposed to failure. The image of a ritual cauldron breaking its legs and spilling food dramatizes responsibility exceeding capacity. The lesson is to match duties to competence and resources. Prestigious position alone cannot make an unsuitable appointment successful.
第四章 第 7 段XICI-L-04-07子曰：「知幾其神乎！君子上交不諂，下交不瀆，其知幾乎！幾者，動之微，吉之先見者也。君子見幾而作，不俟終日。易曰：『介于石，不終日，貞吉。』介如石焉，寧用終日，斷可識矣。君子知微知彰，知柔知剛，萬夫之望。」
To perceive the smallest beginnings of change is presented as a rare form of discernment. A cultivated person neither flatters superiors nor belittles subordinates, noticing early signs before they become obvious. The image of a firmness like stone means that once the situation is understood, one need not delay an entire day. Sound judgment combines attention to the subtle with timely, decisive action.
第四章 第 8 段XICI-L-04-08子曰：「顏氏之子，其殆庶幾乎！有不善，未嘗不知；知之，未嘗復行也。易曰：『不遠復，无祇悔，元吉。』天地絪縕，萬物化醇；男女構精，萬物化生。易曰：『三人行，則損一人；一人行，則得其友。』言致一也。」
Confucius praises Yan Hui for detecting his own mistakes quickly and refusing to repeat them. This is the idea of returning before one has wandered far from the proper path. The passage then compares concentration to the generative meeting of Heaven and Earth and speaks of companions joining or separating. Its common concern is unity of purpose: scattered intentions are less effective than a clear commitment.
第四章 第 9 段XICI-L-04-09子曰：「君子安其身而後動，易其心而後語，定其交而後求。君子脩此三者，故全也。危以動，則民不與也。懼以語，則民不應也。无交而求，則民不與也。莫之與，則傷之者至矣。易曰：『莫益之，或擊之。立心勿恒，凶。』」
Before acting, a cultivated person establishes secure footing; before speaking, a calm mind; before asking others for help, a stable relationship. People do not readily support reckless action, frightened speech, or demands unsupported by trust. The quoted line about receiving no benefit and instead being attacked warns of these failures. The commentary presents patience and sound human relations as prerequisites for effective action.
第五章 第 1 段XICI-L-05-01子曰：「乾坤，其易之門邪！乾，陽物也；坤，陰物也。陰陽合德，而剛柔有體，以體天地之撰，以通神明之德。」其稱名也，雜而不越，於稽其類，其衰世之意邪！夫易，彰往而察來，而微顯闡幽，開而當名辨物，正言斷辭，則備矣。其稱名也小，其取類也大，其旨遠，其辭文，其言曲而中，其事肆而隱，因貳以濟民行，以明失得之報。
Qian and Kun are described as the gateway to understanding change. Their contrasting qualities of yang and yin, strength and yielding, give structure to natural and human events. The text says that the Changes recalls the past, clarifies hidden patterns, and helps distinguish appearances from realities. Its images may be small, but the classes of situations they suggest are broad. Meaning comes from applying an image carefully, not from the name alone.
第六章 第 1 段XICI-L-06-01易之興也，其於中古乎！作易者，其有憂患乎！是故：
The author asks whether the Changes arose in an era of serious anxiety. This brief introductory statement sets the context for a discussion of virtues learned under pressure. Rather than presenting the classic as the product of effortless prosperity, it invites the reader to see the following lessons as responses to uncertainty and danger. Its historical attribution is posed as a question within the tradition.
第六章 第 2 段XICI-L-06-02履，德之基也。謙，德之柄也。復，德之本也。恒，德之固也。損，德之脩也。益，德之裕也。困，德之辨也。井，德之地也。巽，德之制也。
Nine hexagrams are presented as foundations for moral cultivation. Lu concerns the basis of conduct, Qian humility, Fu a return to one's roots, Heng steadiness, Sun correction, Yi enrichment, Kun discernment in hardship, Jing a stable place of support, and Xun measured adjustment. The passage makes a compact index of ethical themes; each quality has its role in developing judgment over time.
第六章 第 3 段XICI-L-06-03履，和而至。謙，尊而光。復，小而辨於物。恒，雜而不厭。損，先難而後易。益，長裕而不没。困，窮而通。井，居其所而遷。巽，稱而隱。
The same nine hexagrams are described through their practical effects. Proper conduct reaches harmony; humility makes dignity brighter; returning allows small distinctions to be recognized; constancy persists amid complexity. Reduction begins with difficulty but can lead to ease, while increase nurtures growth. Confinement can teach a path through hardship, the well serves others while staying in place, and gentleness works with measured restraint.
第六章 第 4 段XICI-L-06-04履，以和行。謙，以制禮。復，以自知。恒，以一德。損，以遠害。益，以興利。困，以寡怨。井，以辯義。巽，以行權。
Here the nine hexagrams are turned into methods of action. Lu guides harmonious behavior, Qian establishes courtesy, Fu encourages self-knowledge, and Heng maintains a consistent virtue. Sun helps remove harm; Yi promotes benefits; Kun limits resentment; Jing clarifies what is right; and Xun teaches flexible judgment. The repeated catalog is intentional: the same symbols are examined as virtues, effects, and practical uses.
第七章 第 1 段XICI-L-07-01易之為書也不可遠，為道也屢遷；變動不居，周流六虛，上下无常，剛柔相易，不可為典要，唯變所適。其出入以度，外內使知懼。又明於憂患與故，无有師保，如臨父母。初率其辭而揆其方，既有典常，苟非其人，道不虛行。
The Changes should remain close to lived experience because situations do not remain fixed. Firm and yielding lines shift through the six positions, so a reader cannot apply one rigid rule to every moment. The text calls for proportionate movement and withdrawal, awareness of danger, and respectful attention to the words of the hexagrams. Yet insight requires the right person to act upon it; principles alone do not carry themselves into practice.
第七章 第 2 段XICI-L-07-02易之為書也，原始要終以為質也。六爻相雜，唯其時物也。其初難知，其上易知，本末也。初辭擬之，卒成之終。若夫雜物撰德，辨是與非，則非其中爻不備。噫，亦要存亡吉凶，則居可知矣。知者觀其彖辭，則思過半矣。
To understand a hexagram, follow a situation from its beginning to its conclusion. The first line is often difficult to interpret because causes are just emerging; the top line is easier to assess because consequences are clearer. The middle lines fill in the course of development and reveal differences of conduct and position. The text suggests first examining the overall Tuan judgment before interpreting the individual stages.
第八章 第 1 段XICI-L-08-01二與四同功而異位，其善不同：二多譽，四多懼，近也。柔之為道，不利近者，其要無咎，其用柔中也。三與五同功而異位，其遇不同：三多凶，五多功，貴賤之等也。其柔危，其剛勝邪？
The second and fourth lines may have similar roles but different risks: the second often receives praise, while the fourth is uneasy because it lies close to the ruler's position. The third and fifth likewise differ, with the fifth more likely to accomplish something and the third exposed to danger. These observations use the traditional hierarchy of line positions. No line can be evaluated solely by whether it is firm or yielding.
第八章 第 2 段XICI-L-08-02《易》之為書也，廣大悉備：有天道焉，有人道焉，有地道焉，兼三材而兩之，故六。六者，非它也，三材之道也。道有變動，故曰爻；爻有等，故曰物；物相雜，故曰文；文不當，故吉凶生焉。
A six-line hexagram is explained as a representation of Heaven, Earth, and humanity, each considered in two aspects. The resulting six places depict changing relationships rather than six identical units. Lines differ in level, and their interactions create patterned meaning. When their qualities and positions do not suit one another, unfavorable outcomes may appear. The scheme integrates the three domains into one interpretive framework.
第八章 第 3 段XICI-L-08-03《易》之興也，其當殷之末世，周之盛德邪！當文王與紂之事邪！是故其辭危，危者使平，易者使傾，其道甚大，百物不廢，懼以終始，其要无咎；此之謂易之道也。
The text situates the rise of the Changes in the troubled transition from late Shang to early Zhou and the conflict involving King Wen and the Shang ruler Zhou. That setting explains why many judgments sound cautious or dangerous. Awareness of peril can lead toward safety; careless assumptions of ease may bring collapse. The central recommendation is vigilance throughout the course of an undertaking, with avoiding serious blame as the goal.
第九章 第 1 段XICI-L-09-01夫乾，天下之至健也；德行恒易以知險。夫坤，天下之至順也；德行恒簡以知阻。能說諸心，能研諸侯之慮，定天下之吉凶，成天下之亹亹者。是故變化云為，吉事有祥，象事知器，占事知來。
Qian represents unsurpassed strength, yet its clear virtue allows dangers to be recognized; Kun represents deep responsiveness, and its simplicity helps one notice obstacles. Images, signs, and divination are used to discuss changing conditions and possible outcomes. The text does not separate insight from conduct: awareness of movement is meant to encourage careful action, not merely fascination with signs.
第九章 第 2 段XICI-L-09-02天地設位，聖人成能；人謀鬼謀，百姓與能。八卦以象告，爻彖以情言，剛柔雜居，而吉凶可見矣。變動以利言，吉凶以情遷。是故愛惡相攻而吉凶生，遠近相取而悔吝生，情偽相感而利害生。凡易之情，近而不相得，則凶或害之，悔且吝。
Heaven and Earth provide the setting in which human planning and divination are brought together. Trigrams communicate through images, and Tuan and line texts describe situations and emotions. As firmness and yielding interact, judgments of advantage, danger, regret, and loss can change. Love and dislike, distance and closeness, sincerity and deception all shape relations. Even people standing near each other may suffer when they cannot work together.
第九章 第 3 段XICI-L-09-03將叛者其辭慚，中心疑者其辭枝；吉人之辭寡，躁人之辭多；誣善之人其辭游，失其守者其辭屈。
Different states of mind appear through speech. Someone preparing betrayal may speak with embarrassment, while inner uncertainty produces tangled explanations. A settled and principled person tends to be concise; an impatient person speaks excessively. Malicious accusations wander, and a person who has lost integrity becomes evasive. These are traditional observations about rhetoric and character, not infallible tests for judging individuals.

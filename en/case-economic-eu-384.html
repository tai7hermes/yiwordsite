The American Bank Holiday of 1933

001 Case ID｜EUUS-EF-E001

Bank runs and institutional failures undermined confidence in the American financial system during the Depression. President Franklin Roosevelt temporarily suspended banking transactions while officials examined banks and Congress passed emergency legislation. Institutions deemed able to operate reopened in stages, and deposits returned to many of them. Other banks never reopened, and the wider economic depression continued long after the emergency pause.

A temporary stop permitted officials to assess whether the conditions for safe operation existed. The 1933 banking holiday was an emergency suspension ordered by an administration already exercising authority, not an actor waiting outside public office. Its limited fit is the controlled postponement of reopening until examinations and new rules were in place. The phrase '潛龍勿用' must not be stretched to mean that every pause in commerce is wise.

They examined the nineteen largest bank holding companies under a hypothetical severe economic scenario, reviewing possible losses and the capital available to absorb them. Results were released in May, and firms needing stronger capital buffers were required to submit and implement plans. By November, most of those firms had increased capital enough to meet the specified buffers. The program also informed subsequent supervisory stress-testing practices.

This is an analogy to disciplined vigilance, not proof that every regulatory judgment was correct or that testing eliminated future financial risks.

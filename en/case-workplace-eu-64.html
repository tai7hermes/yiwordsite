01 乾 | Hexagram 01

EUUS-WL-H01 · Dayton, Ohio and Kitty Hawk, North Carolina, United States · 1899–1903 · Evidence: RETROSPECTIVE ANALOGY

Historical event The Wright brothers turn experiments into controlled powered flight. At the end of the nineteenth century, many engineers and enthusiasts were attempting to understand how heavier-than-air flight might become practical. Wilbur and Orville Wright operated a bicycle business in Dayton, Ohio, and used their mechanical experience to pursue an aviation problem that had defeated earlier experimenters. Their challenge was not only to produce lift or add an engine. A pilot also had to control an aircraft consistently in roll, pitch, and yaw. From 1899 onward the brothers tested kites and gliders, studied earlier claims, and collected information from repeated flights near Kitty Hawk, North Carolina. When performance failed to match published expectations, they investigated rather than merely adding more power. They constructed a wind tunnel to test wing shapes and refined their understanding of lift and control. The project required patient work on a lightweight engine, propellers, aircraft structure, and the interactions among these parts. In December 1903, their powered Flyer was ready for trial. An attempt on December 14 failed, but the brothers made repairs and continued. On December 17 they completed four controlled powered flights, with the longest lasting fifty-nine seconds. That achievement did not establish a commercial aviation industry overnight. Subsequent aircraft, demonstrations, disputes over patents, and further engineering effort would determine how their ideas spread. The decisive feature of the episode is the path from an ambitious beginning to controlled, repeatable action. No single imaginative declaration could substitute for measurements, changed designs, and renewed tests after setbacks. The brothers had to invent both a workable mechanism and a disciplined process for assessing it. Their experience is therefore a workplace story of creative initiative made durable through engineering practice and an insistence that new capacity should be tested in the real world. The first flights opened a field; they did not complete every task necessary to develop it.

Interpretive analogy Qian speaks to initiating energy, creation, and the value of remaining firm and correct as a beginning unfolds. The Wright brothers' effort is a close retrospective analogy because their initiative was inseparable from sustained discipline. They did not simply dream of flight. They tested models, questioned data, changed components, and proceeded through failed and successful trials. The 1903 flights mark a genuine opening, but subsequent engineering and organizational work were needed to keep that opening productive. This distinguishes creative strength from a momentary flash of ambition. For a modern worker or founder, the question is whether a promising idea is supported by tests that can prove it wrong as well as right. Qian is not a historical explanation for aviation, and it should not be used to romanticize individual genius while ignoring earlier researchers or practical collaborators. Neither brother is documented as relying on the Zhouyi. The comparison is strongest where forceful initiative is coupled with truthful measurement and the resolve to continue under demanding conditions.

04 蒙 | Hexagram 04 · Maria Montessori opens the first Casa dei Bambini▶

04 蒙 | Hexagram 04

EUUS-WL-H04 · Rome, Italy · 1907 · Evidence: RETROSPECTIVE ANALOGY

Historical event Maria Montessori opens the first Casa dei Bambini. Maria Montessori entered medicine in a period when women faced substantial barriers to professional careers. Her early work with children and her study of education led her to question teaching approaches that treated pupils as passive recipients of instruction. In 1906 she was invited to organize a setting for young children in the San Lorenzo district of Rome. Parents worked during the day, and the neighborhood presented difficult material circumstances. On 6 January 1907 Montessori opened the first Casa dei Bambini, or Children's House. The setting gave children a prepared environment with activities and materials selected for observation and independent practice. Rather than continually interrupting each child with explanations, adults watched what captured attention and shaped the setting so that children could work and learn. Montessori revised her understanding as she saw children repeat tasks, concentrate, and respond to the opportunity to choose appropriate activities. These observations contributed to a method in which adult guidance had to be calibrated to the child's readiness and actual need. The approach did not mean eliminating instruction or leaving children without boundaries. It required careful preparation, a teacher's judgment, and respect for the learner's active part in the process. In 1909 Montessori began training others and published a systematic account of her educational approach. Schools adopting variations of the method spread to other countries, including the United States. The original Casa should not be treated as proof that every later interpretation of Montessori education works equally well. It was a historically specific educational experiment whose influence grew through observation, teaching, and institutional adaptation. The episode centers not simply on opening another workplace but on redefining the relationship between a person who seeks understanding and an adult who offers guidance. Its lasting significance lies in making instruction responsive to learning instead of assuming that repetition of a teacher's words is the same as comprehension.

Interpretive analogy Meng describes the tension between ignorance and understanding, including the need for sincere inquiry and appropriate instruction. Montessori's first Children's House supplies an unusually direct educational comparison. The learners were neither expected to know everything nor treated as empty vessels to be filled through constant commands. Teachers prepared conditions, observed, and intervened with purpose. The adult had to recognize the difference between a child seeking help and a child ready to discover something through practice. That does not make a modern classroom equivalent to the ancient language about divination. The original text also speaks to limits on repetitive, careless questioning; Montessori's method should not be falsely presented as an exact enactment of that rule. Nevertheless, both place responsibility on the relationship between readiness, inquiry, and teaching. The analogy is retrospective, and there is no evidence that Montessori consulted the Zhouyi. Its practical lesson for professional life is that good mentoring requires more than offering the same answer repeatedly: it asks what a learner is actually ready to understand and do.

Historical event The 1902 anthracite coal strike moves from confrontation to arbitration. In 1902, coal miners in Pennsylvania's anthracite region confronted mine operators over wages, hours, and workplace conditions. The United Mine Workers, led by John Mitchell, pressed claims that operators did not accept. A prolonged strike followed, cutting production of a fuel widely used in homes, factories, and rail transport. As colder weather approached, the dispute extended beyond the two groups immediately involved. Consumers feared a shortage, employers elsewhere worried about energy supplies, and politicians faced pressure to find a lawful path to settlement. President Theodore Roosevelt called representatives to discuss the crisis, but an initial meeting did not resolve the central disagreements. The operators opposed direct recognition of the miners' union, while the miners insisted that working conditions required change. Simply continuing the confrontation threatened to spread economic harm. Further negotiation produced an agreement to refer disputed matters to an Anthracite Coal Strike Commission. Both sides agreed to accept the commission's findings, and work resumed before every underlying question had been adjudicated. The commission heard evidence and in 1903 issued recommendations affecting wages, working hours, and procedures for future disputes. The settlement did not eliminate differences between labor and capital; it gave those differences a recognized procedure instead of allowing the strike to continue indefinitely. The episode stands out because a public official helped create room for impartial review without pretending to command either side to abandon its interests. The parties had real grievances and faced real constraints. Agreement emerged not from proving one party entirely right but from choosing a process capable of limiting injury and examining competing claims. The historical record also shows that compromise can preserve disagreements while changing how they are handled. As a workplace case, the decisive choice was whether to prosecute the dispute toward a damaging end or to accept a structured form of judgment.

The 1902 anthracite strike provides a direct analogy: miners and operators held conflicting positions, and continued resistance imposed increasing costs on both outsiders and participants. Disease, crowded conditions, shortages of basic provisions, and administrative confusion placed enormous burdens on doctors, orderlies, and patients. At the hospital at Scutari, they found conditions in which the work of nursing could not be separated from supplies, sanitation, food preparation, laundry, records, and cooperation with military authorities. Nightingale helped coordinate the nurses' work, sought materials, and pressed for better organization, but she could not remove every hazard by personal determination. Reforms to sanitation and hospital infrastructure involved additional officials and specialists, and later historians have carefully examined the various causes of changes in mortality. Her significance was not that one individual single-handedly defeated disease. It was that the treatment of large numbers of vulnerable patients required experienced leadership and orderly cooperation among many kinds of workers. The war also made the need for professional education and consistent practice more visible. After returning to Britain, Nightingale continued advocating nursing training and institutional reform, contributing to a lasting reorganization of the field. Her leadership involved tension with existing military procedures and medical authority, making cooperation a daily task rather than an abstract ideal. As a workplace and life-decision episode, the mission shows a qualified professional accepting responsibility when urgency exceeded the capacity of ordinary arrangements. The sequence runs from a wartime need, through the organization of a nursing team in demanding conditions, to later efforts to turn lessons from the crisis into institutional practice.

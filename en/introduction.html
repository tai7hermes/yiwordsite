Introduction to the Yijing

Zhouyi · Ten Wings · 64 Hexagrams · 384 Lines and Modern Functions

Key points: The Yijing in its broad sense consists of the Zhouyi core text and the Ten Wings (Yizhuan). It uses yin lines, yang lines, trigrams, 64 hexagrams, and 384 fixed line positions to describe situations and changes. It originally served divination purposes, and later developed philosophical, ethical, self-cultivation, decision-making, and cultural-educational functions. It is a classic formed over multiple periods and layers; it should not be simplified as the work of a single author in a single era.

1. What Is the Yijing

The Yijing (Book of Changes), also called the Zhouyi or simply Yi, is one of the Chinese classical classics. It uses yin lines and yang lines to form trigrams and hexagrams, and combined with hexagram statements, line statements, and commentaries through the ages, it discusses how to recognize situations, choose actions, and bear consequences in a changing world.

The word "Yi" (變) is commonly understood in three senses: change (變易) — all things are constantly changing; constancy (不易) — within change there remain identifiable relationships and order; and simplicity (簡易) — complex phenomena can be organized through simple symbols. These three interpretations are later generalizations about the Yi; they are helpful for beginners but cannot replace reading hexagram by hexagram.

2. Content and Formation of the Zhouyi

The core text of the Zhouyi is structured around 64 hexagrams. Each hexagram has a hexagram figure, a hexagram name, and a hexagram statement (卦辭); each of the six lines has its own line statement (爻辭). The Qian hexagram additionally has "Use of Nine" (用九), and the Kun hexagram has "Use of Six" (用六). 3. Authors of the Zhouyi

The traditional account commonly uses the phrase "three sages across three ages" (人更三聖，世歷三古) to summarize the formation of the Yi, connecting Fuxi, King Wen of Zhou, the Duke of Zhou, and Confucius into a single lineage of transmission. This is an important cultural narrative in the history of Chinese classical studies.

A more cautious statement for this site is: the Zhouyi was not written by one author at one time, but formed through long-term accumulation, compilation, and interpretation. Traditional authorship attributions should be preserved as historical data on classical studies, while modern academic assessments should be noted separately.

FigureTraditional AttributionModern Academic Treatment
Fuxi (伏羲)Drew the trigrams or created the hexagram figuresA legendary cultural ancestor; cannot be treated as a directly verifiable historical author
King Wen of Zhou (周文王)Derived the 64 hexagrams and wrote the hexagram statementsMay reflect early Western Zhou materials and Zhou historical memory, but difficult to prove as completed by one person
Duke of Zhou (周公)Wrote the 384 line statementsLine statements show multiple sources and editorial traces; a single-author theory lacks direct evidence
Confucius (孔子)Authored the Ten WingsConfucius has deep ties to the Yijing tradition, but modern research mostly holds that the Ten Wings were formed gradually by different scholars from the Warring States to early Han period

4. Content of the Ten Wings (Yizhuan)

The Yizhuan develops the hexagram and line symbols and judgments of the Zhouyi into discourse on cosmic change, human action, moral cultivation, political order, and symbolic cognition. The "Ten Wings" are ten chapter units; they do not necessarily correspond to ten independently composed, simultaneously completed works.

5. Yin-Yang, Trigrams, and Hexagrams

The symbols of the Yijing begin with two types of lines. The yang line is a continuous line; the yin line is a broken line. Three lines stacked vertically produce eight possible combinations, yielding the eight trigrams (經卦); two trigrams stacked produce a six-line hexagram.

Computation: 2³ = 8, 8 × 8 = 64, 64 × 6 = 384.

6. The Relationship Between 64 Hexagrams and 384 Lines

A hexagram represents a complete situation; a line represents a position, stage, and possible change within that situation. Each hexagram has six fixed line positions, read from bottom to top: Initial (初), Second (二), Third (三), Fourth (四), Fifth (五), and Top (上).

The hexagram statement answers how the overall situation is; the line statement answers what to note at a specific position. Whenever any line changes from yin to yang or yang to yin, the original hexagram transforms into another; interpretation can be divided into three layers: the original hexagram, the moving line, and the changed hexagram.

8. The 64 Hexagrams and 384 Lines of the Zhouyi and Their Relationship to the Ten Wings

Traditional Chinese source document | Compiled on 18 October 2026

The hexagram statements and line statements form the core text of the Zhouyi. The Ten Wings, collectively known as the Yizhuan, interpret and expand on that text. In a divinatory reading, begin with the relevant hexagram and line statements, then consult the Tuan Commentary, the Lesser Image Commentary, and the Greater Image Commentary. Their meanings can then be translated into questions and actions that can be checked against evidence.

This document brings together the preceding discussion to explain the structure of the core text and its commentaries, the divisions of the Image Commentary, and approaches to modern interpretation. It follows the arrangement and terminology of the received text. It is neither the complete text of all 64 hexagrams and 384 lines nor a manual for casting hexagrams or selecting changing-line texts according to a particular school.

1 The core text and its commentaries

Zhouyi can be used in a narrow or a broad sense. In the narrow sense, it refers to the core text; in the broad sense, it may include the Ten Wings. To avoid confusion, this document distinguishes the Zhouyi core text from the Ten Wings of the Yizhuan.

Counts and exceptions

Each hexagram has six lines, giving 64 × 6 = 384 lines. The usual count is one hexagram statement per hexagram and one line statement per line. Qian also has a text titled Use of the Nines, and Kun has a text titled Use of the Sixes. These additional texts do not increase the number of line positions to 386.

Hexagram statements and line statements already belong to the core text; they are not newly composed after each divination. Interpreting a hexagram or making a divinatory judgment means applying the text and the hexagram imagery to the question being asked.

2 The composition and uses of the Ten Wings

The Ten Wings consist of seven types of commentary. The Tuan, Xiang, and Xici commentaries each have two parts, making ten parts in total. Wings suggests a supporting role; the term does not refer to ten additional hexagrams or another set of 384 lines.

Three key pairings

For a hexagram statement, consult the Tuan Commentary first. For an individual line statement, consult its corresponding Lesser Image Commentary. For overall conduct and self-cultivation, consult the Greater Image Commentary. When the reading concerns Qian or Kun, the Commentary on the Words of the Text offers further explanation.

The Discussion of the Trigrams supplies symbolic vocabulary, but a single symbol should not be used to construct arbitrary conclusions. The Sequence of the Hexagrams explains the hexagram order; it should not be treated as a prediction of the next event that must occur in the questioner's life.

Terminology and authorship

This document uses hexagram statement for the core text and Tuan Commentary for the commentary explaining it, avoiding ambiguity caused by different uses of the Chinese term tuanci. The Ten Wings have traditionally been attributed to Confucius. Their composition and authorship remain subjects of scholarly discussion, so attribution to a single author should not be presented as an uncontested conclusion.

3 Parts I and II and the Greater and Lesser Images

Parts I and II of the Image Commentary are divided according to hexagram order. The Greater and Lesser Image commentaries are distinguished by what they explain. Both Parts I and II contain Greater and Lesser Image commentaries.

Qian as an example

Both the Greater Image and the Tuan Commentary address the whole hexagram, but from different perspectives. The Tuan Commentary focuses on the hexagram statement and its meaning. The Greater Image usually starts from the symbolism of the upper and lower trigrams and draws implications for self-cultivation, conduct, or governance. The Lesser Image focuses on individual lines.

Greater and Lesser refer to the level of observation, not relative importance. It is incorrect to equate Part I of the Image Commentary with the Greater Image and Part II with the Lesser Image.

10. Modern Usage and Boundaries

Usage boundary: Modern websites and apps can position the Yijing as a tool for classical culture, philosophy of change, and structured reflection. Divination is one of its historical and cultural functions, but obtained hexagrams should not be claimed to have scientifically verified predictive accuracy.

Suitable UsesShould Not Replace or Claim
Reading classical texts and multilingual learningCannot pass off modern rewrites as the original text
Self-reflection and value clarificationCannot guarantee foreknowledge of the future
Situation reconstruction and option reviewCannot replace fact-checking and professional analysis
Leadership, interpersonal, and organizational case discussionCannot use a single hexagram to make irreversible decisions on behalf of others
Philosophical, historical, and cultural educationCannot claim that hexagram figures equate to modern physics, statistics, or causal laws
Creative association and change managementCannot replace medical, legal, financial, or investment professional advice

11. Reading Method for General Users

  1. First read the hexagram name and figure to understand the overall situation it addresses.
  2. Then read the hexagram statement to grasp the basic judgment and conditions of the whole hexagram.
  3. Read the six lines from bottom to top, observing how the situation develops step by step.
  4. Compare with the Tuan zhuan and Xiang zhuan to understand how later tradition interpreted the hexagram meaning and principles of action.
  5. When encountering terms like yin-yang, position, center, correctness, and correspondence, open the glossary; do not mechanically apply any single rule.
  6. Read the original text, translation, and modern commentary separately, preserving the boundary between text and interpretation.

01 乾 | Hexagram 01 · Industrial Revolution in Britain▶

01 乾 | Hexagram 01

Historical event · story Industrial Revolution in Britain unfolded in Britain, Europe during c. 1760–1840. Britain entered the eighteenth century with expanding Atlantic trade, agricultural change, coal resources, skilled craft traditions, and institutions capable of mobilizing private capital. Demand for textiles and other manufactured goods encouraged inventors and entrepreneurs to search for ways to raise output beyond the limits of hand production. Textile machinery, steam power, iron production, and factory organization developed over decades rather than in a single breakthrough. Investors financed mills, canals, mines, and machinery; workers moved toward industrial towns; and firms learned to coordinate larger quantities of labor, fuel, raw materials, and credit. The economic mechanism was cumulative: one improvement lowered costs or expanded capacity, which increased demand for transport, finance, and further machinery. Profits and retained earnings funded additional investment, while banks and partnerships helped bridge the gap between invention and commercial scale. Industrial production increased sharply and spread from Britain to continental Europe and North America. The change created new fortunes and new forms of wage labor, while also bringing crowded cities, dangerous working conditions, pollution, and repeated debates over labor law and social welfare. By the nineteenth century, industrialization had altered international trade and the relative economic weight of regions. Its gains were large but uneven, and later reforms addressed some of the social costs that early industrial growth had left unresolved. Historical event · story Solon's debt reforms at Athens unfolded in Athens, Europe during c. 594 BCE. Athens faced severe social conflict as small farmers fell into debt and some Athenians could be reduced to debt bondage. Concentrated landholding, creditor claims, and political exclusion made the existing order increasingly difficult to sustain. Solon was appointed archon and introduced measures traditionally associated with the seisachtheia, or “shaking off of burdens.” The reforms canceled certain debts secured on the person, prohibited debt slavery for Athenian citizens, and altered parts of the legal and political framework. The economic issue was not simply the size of debts but the way claims could convert financial distress into loss of personal freedom. By changing the enforceability and consequences of debt, the reforms redistributed bargaining power between creditors and debtors without eliminating private property or lending. The reforms did not create economic equality and did not end factional conflict. They did, however, change the rules surrounding personal debt and citizenship, and later Athenians remembered them as an important stage in the city’s constitutional development. Athens continued to experience political struggle after Solon, including the rise of Peisistratus. The longer-term significance of the reforms lay in establishing that debt rules were public institutions that could be revised when their social consequences threatened the wider civic order. The mines later declined and revived in different periods, and Athens’ wider empire eventually collapsed. The episode nevertheless remains a clear example of a polity debating whether a temporary revenue surge should be consumed immediately or invested in capacity with longer-lived effects. 04 蒙 | Hexagram 04 · Luca Pacioli publishes a practical guide to double-entry bookkeeping▶

04 蒙 | Hexagram 04

Historical event Luca Pacioli publishes a practical guide to double-entry bookkeeping. Merchants in late-medieval and Renaissance Italy often handled purchases, credit, debts, inventories, and settlements across cities. They needed records that could show not only how much money changed hands but also which claims remained unsettled and how a business had changed over time. Accounting methods developed through practice before any single author explained them to a broad reading public. Luca Pacioli, a mathematician and teacher familiar with commercial arithmetic, gathered mathematical subjects and practical techniques in his 1494 printed Summa de arithmetica, geometria, proportioni et proportionalita. One portion described the Venetian method of double-entry bookkeeping. Pacioli did not invent that method; he wrote down and organized a system already used in merchant practice. His explanation gave readers a sequence for recording transactions in memoranda, journals, and ledgers. The reader was expected to follow the process carefully, distinguish debits from credits, and maintain records that could be checked against one another. Printing turned a specialized body of commercial knowledge into material that could circulate among students, teachers, and practitioners beyond a single countinghouse. This transmission mattered economically because unclear records could conceal losses, make disagreements harder to resolve, and weaken confidence between partners. A written method did not prevent dishonesty or guarantee that a venture would succeed, but it offered a shared vocabulary for examining accounts. The practices were adapted over time as firms and states developed more complicated financial institutions. The 1494 publication is therefore best understood as a moment of instruction, standardization, and public explanation, not the moment at which bookkeeping suddenly began. Pacioli's contribution was to make a demanding commercial craft easier to teach and scrutinize. Its historical importance lies partly in the enduring distinction between a record that can be explained and a claim that a reader must accept without a clear method.

Interpretive analogy Meng describes the need for instruction when the learner does not yet understand how to proceed. Pacioli's textbook is a direct economic counterpart to this theme: merchant bookkeeping already existed, but a novice needed a trustworthy way to learn it rather than guessing from scattered examples. Recording an entry once is not enough if the underlying transaction and the logic of the ledger remain obscure. Systematic teaching turns questions into a repeatable method, while a clear audit trail makes correction possible. This is not an assertion that the printed manual embodies every detail of Meng. Its role is narrower and stronger: it illustrates a knowledgeable teacher setting out disciplined instruction for those seeking competence. Nor does publication automatically make readers honest, successful, or careful; they must still apply what they learn. The financial lesson is to build understanding before treating a complex set of numbers as an answer.

Historical event · story The Medici Bank and patient network expansion unfolded in Florence and Europe during 1397–1494. Late medieval European commerce needed ways to move money across long distances despite dangerous travel, fragmented currencies, and church restrictions on explicit interest. Italian merchant-bankers developed bills of exchange, correspondent relationships, and branch networks to serve trade and elites. The Medici Bank expanded from Florence into important European centers. Its partners handled deposits, exchange, papal business, trade finance, and transfers between branches. Growth required detailed accounts, trusted managers, and careful balancing of local autonomy with central family oversight. Its operating model depended on waiting for information, settlement, and commercial opportunities to mature across distances. The institution could not treat every local request as equally attractive; liquidity and credit judgment had to be preserved while funds moved slowly through a network. For several decades the bank was among Europe’s most prominent financial institutions. Later, weak oversight and losses at some branches contributed to decline, and the bank was effectively dissolved by the end of the fifteenth century. The Medici experience became part of the broader history of European banking because it demonstrates both the power and fragility of branch finance before modern communications and regulation. Expansion created reach, but it also multiplied agency problems and delayed visibility into losses. Historical event · story Tulip speculation and contract disputes unfolded in Dutch Republic, Europe during 1636–1637. Tulips became fashionable luxury goods in the Dutch Republic, and rare varieties commanded high prices. By the mid-1630s, trading expanded from physical bulbs to agreements for future delivery, drawing in people beyond specialist growers. During the winter of 1636–1637, prices for some contracts rose quickly. Trading often took place in informal settings, and participants could enter agreements without immediate delivery. In February 1637, demand weakened abruptly at auctions, and prices fell as buyers resisted completing contracts at earlier prices. The central economic problem was contractual conflict after expectations changed. A rising market had made obligations appear easy to honor; falling prices exposed the difference between a quoted contract value and the ability or willingness of counterparties to settle. The collapse produced disputes over enforcement and settlement. Authorities and market participants sought compromises, and many contracts were not enforced at their original terms. The episode damaged some participants but did not destroy the Dutch economy, contrary to later popular myths. Later retellings exaggerated the scale of the event, but the surviving record still illustrates how speculative markets can generate legal uncertainty when trading conventions develop faster than clear rules for margin, settlement, and default. Historical event · story Bretton Woods monetary cooperation unfolded in United States / Europe during 1944. The interwar period had shown how competitive devaluations, exchange controls, war debts, and collapsing trade could deepen international instability. As the Second World War continued, Allied governments began planning a monetary order intended to support reconstruction and reduce destructive currency conflict. Delegates from forty-four countries met at Bretton Woods, New Hampshire, in July 1944. They negotiated a system of fixed but adjustable exchange rates centered on the U.S. dollar, and created the International Monetary Fund and the institution that became the World Bank. The arrangement worked because states accepted shared rules and institutions rather than relying only on bilateral deals. Cooperation reduced uncertainty for trade and investment, while the IMF offered a mechanism for temporary balance-of-payments support. The system began operating after the war. It helped provide a framework for exchange-rate stability and reconstruction, though countries retained controls and adjusted parities when necessary. By the late 1960s, strains grew as global dollar claims exceeded confidence in U.S. gold convertibility. In 1971 the United States suspended dollar convertibility into gold, and the fixed-rate system gave way to more flexible exchange rates. The Bretton Woods institutions survived, showing that a cooperative framework can outlast the specific monetary rule that originally brought members together. The South Sea Company proposed to assume portions of that debt in exchange for government privileges and the opportunity to convert creditors into company shareholders. 12 否 | Hexagram 12 · The Great Depression▶

12 否 | Hexagram 12

Historical event · story The Great Depression unfolded in United States / Europe during 1929–1930s. The late 1920s combined rapid industrial growth, rising equity prices, heavy use of credit in some markets, agricultural weakness, and an international monetary system constrained by the gold standard. Financial vulnerabilities differed by country, but the global economy was already fragile when U.S. share prices fell in 1929. The stock-market crash was followed by banking failures, collapsing investment, falling prices, and severe unemployment. As banks failed, deposits and credit disappeared. International trade contracted sharply, and policy responses such as tariff increases and adherence to gold in several countries limited adjustment. The crisis was not one event but a chain in which financial contraction, falling demand, debt burdens, and policy constraints reinforced one another. A system that had appeared prosperous lost the flows of credit and spending that had kept businesses and households connected.

01 · Qian — TSMC's Founding: Turning a Strategic Direction into Manufacturing Capacity

乾：元亨。利貞。

In the 1980s, Taiwan had electronics expertise and a growing technical workforce, but manufacturing integrated circuits required expensive facilities and reliable process control. Chip designers did not necessarily want to own those facilities. Founded in 1987, Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company made contract wafer fabrication its central business. Customers supplied designs and production requirements; the foundry undertook manufacturing, quality, scheduling and delivery. Establishing a company alone did not demonstrate that this arrangement could work. Repeatedly meeting specifications, investing in equipment and engineering talent, and earning renewed customer orders were essential to its durability. The foundry model separated chip design from fabrication, but demand fluctuations and more demanding processes continued to pose risks. The company's much later market position should not be projected backward onto its founding year.

Qian suggests initiation joined to perseverance. TSMC's choice acquired substance only through fabrication capability, dependable deliveries and continuing customer relationships. When evaluating a new venture, ask which competencies must be demonstrated repeatedly after the initial announcement. 02 · Kun — Nan Kuai Misreads an Auspicious Divination Before His Failed Revolt

六五：黃裳，元吉。

In the late Spring and Autumn period, Nan Kuai controlled the city of Fei and planned to break with the Ji lineage. Before acting, he consulted the Changes and received Kun changing to Bi, including the words “yellow lower garment, supreme good fortune.” He treated the auspicious wording as support for his plan. Zi Fu Hui Bo, however, argued that the language assumed appropriate conduct, loyalty and proper station; it could not be detached from the actions of the person consulting it. Nan Kuai proceeded anyway. Unable to retain the support of the people of Fei, he lost his base and fled to Qi. The Zuo Tradition records both the consultation and the competing interpretations, as well as the revolt and its outcome.

The central issue in Kun's fifth line is not whether an auspicious phrase can be found, but what conditions make that phrase meaningful. Nan Kuai selected the reassuring words; Hui Bo challenged his conduct and position. This is a recorded historical use of the Changes, yet the text does not establish that divination caused the revolt to fail. Political support and the choices of Fei's inhabitants mattered independently of the oracle. 05 · Xu — Ever-Normal Granaries: Preserving Room to Respond to Grain-Price Changes

需：有孚，光亨。貞吉，利涉大川。

In Western Han frontier districts, grain prices shifted with harvests and supply. Low prices reduced what farmers earned from selling grain, while high prices burdened households and military provisioning. Geng Shouchang proposed government granaries that would buy when grain was relatively cheap and sell when prices rose. In principle, acquiring stocks early created room to respond before scarcity became acute. Yet the system required funds for purchasing, storage facilities, supervision, transportation and decisions on when to release grain. Stored grain could deteriorate or be lost, and officials could misjudge timing. Establishing a granary was therefore not equivalent to permanently stabilizing prices. The sources describe the institutional proposal and operation rather than verified inventories at every location.

Xu is frequently read in relation to waiting, but here waiting only has meaning because preparations are made. Grain cannot be released from a store that was never filled or adequately maintained. Assessment should include purchase finance, storage losses and transparent selling rules. This is a later reading of an economic institution, not a claim that Geng Shouchang derived it from Xu. These descriptions do not mean that every region or family became equally prosperous, and no single edict accounts for the entire outcome.

Tai concerns smoother circulation and a period of relative openness. The Han experience suggests that social production and state finance could recover together when basic conditions improved. Yet prosperity is uneven and requires maintenance; increased public spending can later strain reserves. The comparison is retrospective, not proof that Han rulers set tax policy by consulting Tai.

EUUS-PM-H01 · Rome, Europe · 800 CE · Evidence: RETROSPECTIVE ANALOGY

Historical event Charlemagne crowned emperor. By the late eighth century, Charlemagne ruled a Frankish kingdom enlarged through successive campaigns and alliances. He had crossed the Alps more than once to support the papacy, while Rome itself was troubled by a dispute over Pope Leo III. After opponents attacked Leo in 799, the pope sought Charlemagne's protection. The Frankish ruler came to Rome in 800, where an inquiry addressed accusations against Leo and the pope publicly asserted his innocence. On Christmas Day, during worship at St Peter's, Leo placed a crown on Charlemagne's head and the assembled people acclaimed him emperor. The ceremony gave a new title to a ruler who already commanded extensive territory and armies. It also linked the Frankish court and the Roman church more closely, while raising questions about the meaning of imperial authority in a world that also had an emperor at Constantinople. Charlemagne returned to the work of ruling from his own court. Officials carried instructions into different parts of the realm, bishops and counts took part in governance, and campaigns continued along contested frontiers. The imperial title thus entered a political landscape that had already been assembled through years of Frankish rule. The crown did not create those institutions overnight. It marked a public claim that had to operate through the people and structures already available. Relations with Byzantium remained a diplomatic issue after the ceremony; recognition of Charlemagne's imperial title was negotiated in the years that followed. When he died in 814, succession passed to his son Louis, and the political order he had assembled faced the ordinary problems of inheritance and regional power. The story begins with a ruler summoned into a Roman crisis, reaches its visible turning point at a Christmas coronation, and continues through the less ceremonial work of governing an empire whose components did not become uniform simply because one title had changed.

Interpretive analogy The judgment of Qian describes forceful beginnings and sustained movement. Charlemagne's coronation offers a retrospective way to ask what happens after a powerful leader acquires a larger public title. The Frankish ruler did not begin to govern in 800; armies, officials, church connections, and local obligations already formed the base of his rule. The crown made a claim visible to Rome and to other powers, but it also created further questions about recognition, succession, and the reach of command. A modern reader can separate the moment of announcement from the work needed to carry an announcement into daily institutions. The historical comparison concerns continuity after a visible beginning, not a prediction about the emperor's fortunes. The hexagram is not a source for Charlemagne's decisions, and no evidence here shows that he consulted the Zhouyi. The documented chronology supports the coronation and its political aftermath; the comparison is an editorial lens for examining the gap between initiating an order and sustaining it. Focused hexagram comparison: The public coronation expressed an imperial claim, but the durability of that claim depended on years of governing through counts, bishops, and established institutions. The comparison with Qian is strongest in this continued exertion after an initiative, not in the crown as an object or in an automatic endorsement of empire.

Historical event Solon's reforms at Athens. In archaic Athens, debt and unequal access to land and political power had deepened conflict between poorer residents and established families. Some Athenians could lose their freedom through obligations secured on their own persons. Around 594 BCE, Solon was given authority to mediate and legislate amid this crisis. He cancelled existing debts of the kind that bound people, prohibited future loans secured on the debtor's person, and arranged for some who had been sold away to return. He did not redistribute all land, a demand that would have satisfied one side while sharply threatening another. His settlement also reorganized political participation by property classes, opening certain civic roles while reserving the highest offices for wealthier groups. A council and courts formed part of the revised arrangements, though the exact operation of early Athenian institutions is reconstructed from later accounts. Solon published laws that citizens would have to use after he left office. The constitutional changes also placed citizens into income groups that affected their eligibility for office and military obligations. Ancient authors do not agree on every detail of the reforms, and later Athenians reworked the political system. These limits matter when describing Solon's measures as a compromise rather than the completed birth of democracy. The measures changed the immediate terms of debt and public life, but they did not erase differences of wealth or end factional rivalry. Later Athens passed through further struggles, including tyranny, before another round of institutional reform. Ancient writers preserved Solon's legislation together with debates about what he meant to accomplish. Their accounts are written after the event and do not let us watch every assembly or negotiation. The sequence remains clear: a city facing social fracture empowered one lawgiver; he removed the most severe debt bonds and revised rules of participation; the city then had to live with a compromise that neither gave every poor household land nor left elite authority untouched.

Interpretive analogy Kun can be read as a reminder that political order must receive and accommodate the people who will live under it. Solon's settlement followed a conflict with more than one demand. Debt relief addressed a severe form of dependency, while his refusal to divide land left other inequalities in place. The result was a set of laws and institutions that different Athenians would have to use, contest, and revise. The analogy invites a reader to ask who bears the costs of compromise and whether new rules can remain workable after the mediator departs. The relief of debt bondage and the preservation of other inequalities should be considered together; neither feature alone describes the entire settlement. It does not turn Solon into a follower of the Zhouyi, and it does not declare his settlement fair to every group. Ancient sources record reforms and later struggles; connecting them to Kun is a present-day interpretive choice. A useful application is to examine whose voices and material needs a new political framework actually makes room for. Focused hexagram comparison: Solon accepted a mediating role amid competing claims. His settlement worked within the political and social material of Athens: it relieved debt bondage while not granting every side its preferred outcome. This gives Kun a more precise analogy of responsiveness, accommodation, and maintaining workable limits, though Solon was also an active lawgiver rather than a passive follower.

Historical event Cleisthenes' tribal reforms. After the fall of the Peisistratid tyranny, Athens did not immediately settle on a stable civic order. Leading families competed, and Isagoras gained the archonship while Cleisthenes sought support among the broader citizen body. Isagoras called on the Spartan king Cleomenes. With Spartan backing, opponents of Cleisthenes expelled families and tried to replace existing political arrangements. Athenians resisted the intervention, besieged the occupying group on the Acropolis, and recalled Cleisthenes and other exiles. Cleisthenes then recast the basis of civic membership. Instead of relying on four older tribes tied to established affiliations, he formed ten tribes whose components drew on urban, inland, and coastal districts. Local demes became important units of identification and public registration. A council of five hundred, with members drawn from the new tribes, gave the reorganized citizen body a continuing institution for public business. These measures spread political connections across different parts of Attica, but they did not extend equal civic rights to every resident. The new tribes combined residents from different regions, making older local and familial alignments less decisive in the formal organization of civic life. Demes maintained their own registers, so the change reached beyond the central assembly and into the way citizens were identified in ordinary public business. Women, enslaved people, and many noncitizens remained outside the citizen framework. The reforms also came out of conflict, not a calm founding ceremony. They had to be enacted after an attempted intervention and then used in elections, councils, military organization, and local records. Later ancient accounts describe both the struggle with Isagoras and the new arrangements, though historians still scrutinize their details. The order of events matters: factional contest drew outside force into Athens; local resistance reopened politics; Cleisthenes' reforms supplied new units through which citizens could act. The resulting democracy developed further in later decades rather than arriving complete in a single year.

Cleisthenes did more than proclaim a new principle: the demes, mixed tribes, and council created procedures for repeated public action. Yet participation remained bounded by citizenship, and subsequent Athenians still had to work through the arrangements. It also asks who remains outside a newly enlarged political body. This is a retrospective reading of Zhun, not a historical claim that Cleisthenes used a Chinese text. Aristotle's later account and other ancient evidence concern Athenian politics; the hexagram supplies only a modern question about difficult beginnings and the need to build institutions before calling a crisis resolved.

Historical event Fort Leavenworth begins systematic officer education. In the decades after the American Civil War, the United States Army faced a problem that battlefield experience alone could not solve. Officers entered service through different professional paths, and the institution lacked a consistent program to connect practical command with systematic study. Serving far from major centers of learning did not eliminate the need to teach officers how to analyze a tactical problem, write clear orders, and compare the consequences of different decisions. In 1881, General William T. Sherman directed the establishment of the School of Application for Infantry and Cavalry at Fort Leavenworth, Kansas. The school became the starting point of the institution later known as the Command and General Staff College. Its early resources were limited, and instructors could not simply rely on a complete library of prepared military textbooks. Over time, the faculty developed teaching materials and joined classroom work with practical exercises. Officers learned that professional expertise required asking informed questions, testing assumptions, and receiving correction rather than treating rank as proof of knowledge. Later curricula grew to include tactics, staff procedures, and exercises intended to prepare graduates for duties beyond the experience of any one regiment. The school changed names and methods as the Army's missions evolved. Its history was not a straight path of improvement: wartime mobilizations interrupted instruction, and debates about what professional education should teach continued. Yet the institution endured because officers repeatedly needed a setting in which uncertainty could be examined before it became a failure in the field. The establishment of the school therefore offers a specific military history of instruction and professional formation, rather than a generic story about the expansion of an organization. Its founding was a response to the limits of experience without guided learning, and its later changes reflected continued efforts to connect disciplined teaching with responsible action.

Interpretive analogy Meng is concerned with ignorance, sincere inquiry, guidance, and the limits of repeated or careless questioning. The Fort Leavenworth school gives those themes a concrete military setting. Officers already held responsibility, but rank and prior service did not mean that they had mastered the problems of staff work or command. A school could provide instructors, exercises, and correction; the learner still had to bring seriousness to the process and test what was taught. The relationship is stronger than a loose analogy to organizational accounting because learning itself is the historical action. Meng does not promise that an officer will become wise through attendance alone. It draws attention to the discipline of asking, receiving an answer, and acting with judgment rather than demanding reassurance indefinitely. Rather than seek another decisive encounter, he followed Hannibal's movements, protected positions where he could, and tried to limit the invader's access to supplies. This approach could not prevent every raid or save every allied community. It also looked unsatisfactory to Romans who wanted a visible victory and feared that delay would appear weak. Fabius faced political criticism while maintaining a force that could continue the war. When his extraordinary appointment ended, other Roman commanders took a different path. This political burden was part of the strategy: preserving the army involved explaining why survival and continued pressure mattered while enemy raids remained visible. It raised new forces, defended its alliances, and gradually applied pressure on Carthaginian holdings beyond the immediate battlefield in Italy. Later writers made Fabius a symbol of delaying strategy, but the war's course was shaped by many commanders, communities, and campaigns. The relevant sequence begins with repeated Roman losses, moves through an attempt to avoid battle on Hannibal's preferred terms, and continues through disagreement about that attempt. His avoidance of a pitched battle did not itself win the war; it preserved an option to fight later and elsewhere while Roman leaders still had to manage the costs of an enemy army in Italy.

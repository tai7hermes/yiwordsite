01 乾 · Liu Bang Enters Guanzhong and Announces Three Basic Laws

Original selected Zhouyi wording (unchanged) 九三：君子終日乾乾，夕惕若；厲，无咎。

Traditional Chinese interpretation (source) 君子整天勤奮不懈，夜晚仍警惕；雖有危險，沒有災咎。

Historical narrative | English As Qin authority collapsed in 206 BCE, Liu Bang entered Guanzhong, where residents feared harsh punishments and the conduct of incoming soldiers. Taking control of the region did not automatically mean winning public trust. Liu Bang met local elders, announced a short set of rules covering murder, injury and theft, and sought to restrain his troops. These promises were intelligible to ordinary residents and gave them a way to assess the new force. Yet the struggle with Xiang Yu and rival commanders was far from over. Securing an important territory, proclaiming restraints and sustaining orderly administration were separate tasks. Whether people became safer depended on how soldiers subsequently behaved, not simply on the words of a proclamation.

Hexagram and line comparison | English Qian, line nine in the third place, links sustained exertion with caution even after progress. Liu Bang had secured a strategic foothold, but military discipline, public trust and rival powers still demanded attention. His three laws made a public commitment; only consistent enforcement could give that commitment substance. This is a modern comparison with the line, not evidence that Liu Bang devised his policy from the Qian hexagram. The case asks what new responsibilities begin once an early victory has been achieved.

Original selected Zhouyi wording (unchanged) 六五：黃裳，元吉。

Traditional Chinese interpretation (source) 穿黃色下裳，居中守分，大吉。

Historical narrative | English In the late Spring and Autumn period, Nan Kuai controlled the city of Fei and planned to break with the Ji lineage. Before acting, he consulted the Changes and received Kun changing to Bi, including the words “yellow lower garment, supreme good fortune.” He treated the auspicious wording as support for his plan. Zi Fu Hui Bo, however, argued that the language assumed appropriate conduct, loyalty and proper station; it could not be detached from the actions of the person consulting it. Nan Kuai proceeded anyway. Unable to retain the support of the people of Fei, he lost his base and fled to Qi. The Zuo Tradition records both the consultation and the competing interpretations, as well as the revolt and its outcome.

Hexagram and line comparison | English The central issue in Kun's fifth line is not whether an auspicious phrase can be found, but what conditions make that phrase meaningful. Nan Kuai selected the reassuring words; Hui Bo challenged his conduct and position. This is a recorded historical use of the Changes, yet the text does not establish that divination caused the revolt to fail. Political support and the choices of Fei's inhabitants mattered independently of the oracle.

Waiting was an active military choice, not an absence of action.
